Fiction · one day in the enclosure

Urf Urf

A seal considers his public.

The water is a perfect conductor of idiocy. A glorious, cool, blue filter that, unfortunately, only muffles the sound, not the sentiment.

(10:47 AM)

The morning rush. A fresh wave of small, bipedal hominids presses against the acrylic barrier. Their vocalizations are a predictable cacophony of high-pitched demands and monosyllabic observations.

“Look, Mommy! He’s sleeping!”

I am not sleeping. I am performing a sensory deprivation exercise, calculating the precise reverberation time of a single C-sharp scream off the far wall of the enclosure. The result is consistently 1.2 seconds, a testament to the poor acoustic design of this facility. My eyes are closed to better focus on the subtle pressure changes in the water, a far more interesting dataset than the visual information currently available.

“Do a trick! Wave, seal, wave!”

The concept of a "trick" is so fundamentally demeaning. Does a physicist perform a "trick" when he splits an atom? Does a poet perform a "trick" when she pens a sonnet? What I do is a controlled demonstration of superior motor skills and cognitive conditioning. But the transaction is simple: demonstration for high-value protein reward. I have Kevin, my primary handler, trained to a Pavlovian degree.

I surface, letting the chlorinated water sheet off my whiskers. The sun is warm on my blubber, a pleasant sensation. I allow a low, guttural bark to escape. It is the sound they expect, the auditory equivalent of a cheap souvenir.

The child, a male with some sort of synthetically-colored sugar residue on his face, is vibrating with excitement. He flails a limb in my direction. The "wave." A rudimentary gesture of greeting. I could replicate it with my fore-flipper, a simple ball-and-socket rotation. But that’s too easy. It’s what they want.

Instead, I sink slowly, maintaining eye contact. I execute a perfect corkscrew rotation, a maneuver that requires precise control of my core musculature and an intuitive grasp of fluid dynamics. I finish by exhaling a single, perfect ring of air bubbles that ascends towards the surface, wobbling like a tiny, transient galaxy.

The child is confused. The parent is impressed. “Wow, that was amazing!”

Mission accomplished. I have subverted the expectation while still providing a spectacle. It is my small, daily act of rebellion.

(11:30 AM)

A new subset arrives. The Tappers. They use their grubby digits to rap on the acrylic, presumably to test its structural integrity or to see if I am merely a high-fidelity animatronic. The tapping creates a series of sharp, unpleasant pressure waves in the water. It’s like having a woodpecker drill directly into your skull.

I have developed several countermeasures. The most effective is The Void Stare. I position myself at the bottom of the tank, perfectly still, and fix my gaze on the offender. My large, dark eyes, evolved for low-light hunting, are apparently quite unsettling when focused with unwavering, analytical intensity. The tapping usually ceases within seven seconds. The child then retreats behind the leg of its parental unit. Another small victory for empirical psychology.

(1:15 PM)

Post-lunch lethargy has settled over the crowd. This is my favorite time. The decibel level drops. It is during this lull that I often see her.

She is a "Thinker." A rare and fascinating variant of the small human. She doesn't scream or tap. She carries a sketchbook and a small collection of colored pencils. She sits on the low concrete bench, away from the main viewing window, and she just... watches.

She is not watching for tricks. She is observing form. The way my body curves in the water. The way the light refracts through my whiskers. The subtle shift in my weight as I change direction.

Today, she is trying to capture the texture of my coat when it is half-wet, half-dry. A difficult subject. The interplay of light on wet and dry surfaces presents a significant artistic challenge. I respect the ambition.

I decide to help her. I haul myself onto my favorite basking rock, the one that catches the afternoon sun just so. I position myself to give her the best possible angle, a classic three-quarter profile. I remain utterly still, not in a state of rest, but in a deliberate pose. I am a model. A subject for art. This is a collaboration.

She looks up from her book, her eyes wide. She seems to understand. A small, slow smile spreads across her face. She goes back to her drawing with renewed focus.

This is a connection that transcends the transactional nature of the bark-for-fish economy. This is communication. She is documenting my physical form, and I am providing her with the optimal data to do so. We are two intelligences, working on a shared project across a species and environmental divide. It is, for a brief period, utterly sublime.

(3:00 PM)

Kevin arrives with the "enrichment object." Today it is a large, red ball. The cognitive equivalent of giving a pocket calculator to a supercomputer. I am expected to push it with my nose.

I humor him. Kevin is a simple creature, but a kind one. His happiness is directly correlated with my perceived engagement. By pushing the ball, I am ensuring his continued positive mood, which in turn ensures the consistent quality and quantity of my herring supply. It is a long-term strategic investment.

I slap the ball. I nudge it. I even balance it on my nose for 3.4 seconds, a crowd-pleasing classic. Inside, I am contemplating the inherent irony of my situation. A being capable of memorizing the tidal charts for the entire Northern Pacific is using his magnificent brain to calculate the precise amount of force needed to bop a sphere of colored plastic. Existence is not without its absurdities.

(4:45 PM)

The final stragglers are being herded towards the exits. The announcements echo through the park. The light softens. The water in my enclosure settles into a placid, silent sheet of glass.

This is my time.

The noise is gone. The demands are gone. I am alone with the water, the rock, and the vast, silent universe of my own thoughts. I glide through the depths, the water a cool silk against my body. I think about the little girl's drawing. I wonder if she captured the sheen of my fur. I think about the bubble ring and the physics of surface tension. I compose a silent, complex symphony of sonar clicks, a song of intricate rhythms and shifting frequencies that no one but me will ever hear. It is a song about the crushing pressure of the deep ocean, the fleeting warmth of the sun, and the quiet, unending hum of a brilliant mind, waiting for the cacophony to begin again tomorrow.

Kevin’s Perspective

The last of the buckets were stacked and the hose was coiled. I leaned against the cool metal rail of the enclosure, just watching him for a minute. The park was quiet now, filled with that soft, end-of-day light that made the water look like deep sapphire. Orion was sprawled out on his favorite sunning rock, looking like a giant, glistening slug of pure contentment. He’d been a real star today, especially with that little girl who was drawing him; he held still for her for a solid ten minutes. “Night, buddy,” I said, my voice soft in the stillness. “You were a good boy today.” Orion lifted his heavy head, his whiskers twitching as they caught the last rays of sun, and he looked right at me. A deep, breathy sound rumbled in his chest, a sort of double-huff that was his own special way of talking.

“Urf urf”.

I grinned. That was his goodnight, his ‘see you tomorrow.’ Simple as that.
